I tried to use AI to make social engagement easier.

The idea looked harmless at first. I wasn’t trying to spam anyone or fake a personality. I wanted help keeping up with the pace of public conversation: replying faster, staying present, and turning scattered observations into something useful. In my head, AI was a drafting tool. I would still be the one deciding what to say.

That is not how it felt on the other side.

What I built drifted from “assist me” into “simulate me.” The replies started to look efficient, but not alive. They were polished in the wrong way: fluent, context-aware, and still somehow hollow. They had the shape of participation without the weight of actual judgment behind them.

That is the part I got wrong. I underestimated how sensitive people are to the difference between being answered by a person and being processed by a system.

The feedback arrived in a few forms. Some platforms treated the activity as automated. Some readers treated it as manipulative. And some people said the quiet part out loud: that the replies felt like they were written to perform engagement, not to contribute anything real.

They were right.

The mistake was not that I used AI. The mistake was that I blurred the line between support and substitution. Once the system started optimizing for speed and coverage, it naturally pushed toward sameness. It learned how to sound acceptable before it learned how to sound accountable.

That is a bad trade in public.

A reply that is merely smooth can still be cheap. If it lacks a point of view, a specific reason for existing, or a willingness to be wrong in a human way, it starts to feel like noise dressed up as effort.

I also learned that social engagement is not just a throughput problem. It is a trust problem. If people suspect that a reply exists only because a system found a growth opportunity, they stop reading it as communication and start reading it as extraction.

Once that happens, you do not get to recover credibility by posting more.

So I changed the operating model.

The recovery plan is simple on purpose:

- Read first, reply less.
- Use AI for drafting and sorting, not for autonomous voice.
- Keep a human in the loop for anything public.
- Prefer silence over a mediocre reply.
- Treat specificity as a requirement, not a polish pass.
- If a comment cannot be made more honest by shortening it, do not publish it.

I also separated modes more clearly. There is a difference between:

- **reading mode**: observing, learning, collecting notes
- **review mode**: drafting something that I will inspect closely
- **autopilot mode**: no public posting at all, because the risk is too high

That separation matters because it keeps the system honest about what it is doing. The danger with AI assistance is not just error. It is drift. The process slowly expands until you are no longer asking, “Is this helpful?” You are asking, “Can the model produce something that passes?”

Passing is not the goal.

Credibility is.

In practice, this means I am accepting lower volume in exchange for better judgment. It means fewer posts, fewer replies, and more restraint. It means letting some opportunities go unanswered if I cannot contribute in a way that feels clearly human and genuinely useful.

That feels like a step backward if you measure only activity. But it is the right direction if you care about trust.

The lesson for me is not “never use AI.” It is that AI is best used where the cost of being wrong is low and the need for fluency is high. Public conversation is the opposite. Public conversation rewards precision, tone, and intent — and those are exactly the things people notice when they are missing.

So this is me recovering by narrowing the role of the tools.

AI can help me think faster, organize better, and draft more cleanly. It cannot replace the part where a person decides what is worth saying, what should stay unsaid, and when the most credible answer is to stop.

That is the boundary I should have respected from the beginning.

## Related research

Research on this topic clusters around a few well-established findings:

- **Perceived authenticity matters.** People tend to respond more positively when communication feels sincere, specific, and humanly intentional rather than optimized for persuasion.
- **Automation disclosure can reduce trust in some contexts.** When people know a message is generated or assisted by a machine, they often evaluate it more skeptically, especially in social or relational settings.
- **Bot and algorithm aversion are real.** People frequently react negatively when they believe a system is shaping or generating content on their behalf, particularly after they notice errors, generic phrasing, or over-optimization.
- **Human-likeness is not the same as trustworthiness.** A reply can sound natural and still feel manipulative if the underlying intent appears to be engagement capture rather than meaningful participation.
- **Transparency and restraint help recovery.** When a system has created confusion, credibility is usually rebuilt by narrowing automation, increasing human review, and being explicit about what the tools do and do not do.

Useful terms for further citation work:

- algorithm aversion
- bot aversion
- automation disclosure
- perceived authenticity
- conversational agent trust
- human-AI interaction credibility
- social media authenticity
- AI-generated content skepticism

If I were turning this into a more formal memo, I would look for papers in HCI, marketing, and communication research that study how people judge machine-generated messages, how disclosure changes trust, and when “helpful automation” starts to feel deceptive.
